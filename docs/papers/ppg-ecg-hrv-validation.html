Optical HRV against chest ECG on thirty overnight recordings: near-perfect beat detection, a small rMSSD bias, and a closed-form jitter model that does not hold

Michal Planicka  ·  corresponding author — Tepna Project

Abstract

Background. Draft v1 of this paper compared optical HRV against chest ECG on four overnight sessions and reported rMSSD agreement of −0.6% on the two nights the pipeline's own quality gate trusted; Draft v2 widened that to twenty nights and added a closed-form error model. This draft re-cuts every panel from a committed producer and retracts the model. Data. One subject wore a Polar H10 chest ECG (~130 Hz) and a Polar Verity Sense upper-arm PPG (3 green LED pairs, ~176 Hz) simultaneously; the corpus read here yields 30 paired nights, 2,000 five-minute epochs with a timing pair and 1,961 with an HRV pair (163.4 h). All beats derive from raw signal by the production detectors. Methods. A single global affine time map between the two independent BLE clocks is insufficient — it produces a physiologically impossible negative pulse-transit time — because the devices do not drift at a constant rate and pulse-transit time itself wanders; alignment is therefore re-estimated per epoch. Results. Beat detection is at ceiling: median sensitivity 100.00% (IQR 99.58–100.00) and PPV 100.00%. Pulse-interval timing carries essentially zero bias (+0.01 ms) with a jitter of 6.34 ms (IQR 4.61–8.83). Per-epoch rMSSD runs +4.20% high (per-night median of per-epoch medians; 25 of 30 nights within ±10%, 28 of 30 positive) and SDNN +2.60%, but the night-level spread is wide (mean +12.56%, sd 29.57, limits of agreement −45% to +71%). The central result is a refutation. Draft v2's model rMSSD²ppg = rMSSD²ecg + k·σ² was reported at per-night R² = 0.964 with k = 2.39; re-cut it reads k = 1.21 (IQR 0.44–2.53) and R² = 0.088 (IQR −0.046–0.343), below the theoretical band k ∈ [2,6], and no other denominator (0.227, 0.579), the free two-term fit (a = 1.06, R² = 0.672) or the devices' bare rMSSD agreement (0.649) reproduces the published pair. Part of the reason is structural: within a night the jitter IQR is only 4.82 ms, so σ² has little variance for a per-night fit to explain. What survives is an association, not a law: catastrophic epochs (|ΔrMSSD| > 25%, 14.4% of epochs) carry median jitter 19.2 ms against 5.6 ms elsewhere, and 67% exceed 15 ms against 8% of the rest. Conclusion. Optical rMSSD on this hardware is accurate in the median and unreliable in the tail, and the tail is where the jitter is — but the closed-form budget Draft v2 inverted from this model rests on a k this corpus does not identify, and Table 2 is published as an illustration with its weak fit stated, not as a design target. This is a single-subject method study, not a population accuracy rating.

0. Layman overview (delete before submission)

An armband reads your pulse with light; a chest strap reads the heart’s electricity directly and is the trusted “truth.” We wore both to bed for thirty nights and compared them beat by beat. The light sensor finds essentially every heartbeat — it almost never misses one and almost never invents one. But it places each beat with a small timing wobble of about six thousandths of a second, because it is watching a pressure wave arrive rather than the electrical spark that caused it. A common heart-rhythm score comes out about 4% too high on a typical night, which is small, and on two bad nights it is out by twice the true value, which is not. The previous version of this paper claimed the wobble explained almost all of that error — tightly enough (96%) to turn accuracy into one engineering number. Re-measured with code anyone can run, it does not. The wobble explains under a tenth of the error night by night, and four other ways of asking the same question all give middling answers too. What stays true is cruder and still useful: the nights and the five-minute stretches that go badly wrong are the wobbly ones — three times the wobble on the worst stretches — so reducing wobble is still the right thing to work on. We just cannot say how much accuracy a given reduction buys. One person, thirty nights, and an honest retraction: a method result, not a product rating.

1. Introduction

Heart-rate variability from a photoplethysmogram (PPG) is attractive because the hardware is cheap and unobtrusive, but optical pulse timing is a mechanical, downstream proxy for the electrical heartbeat an ECG records directly. The literature's summary of that gap is largely qualitative — pulse-rate variability is “unbiased but noisier” than heart-rate variability — and the engineering consequence is left implicit. The most recent quantitative synthesis (Xu et al. 2026, a meta-analysis of PRV–HRV agreement on RMSSD and SDNN) pools only resting, controlled-condition studies and explicitly warns that its estimates “should not be generalized to sleep … or free-living settings” — which is precisely the setting this paper measures: overnight sleep, on consumer hardware, every epoch reported. This paper closes that gap on real overnight data by asking not whether optical rMSSD is biased but what quantity produces the bias, and then inverting the relationship into a design budget.

What changed since Draft v1, and what Draft v3 retracts. Draft v1 used four nights and reported rMSSD agreement of −0.6% on the two its reference-free gate trusted — n = 2 for the headline, with the gate evaluated on the same four nights that selected them. Draft v2 used 20 nights, reported every epoch that aligned, and the headline agreement got honestly worse (+4.24%). Draft v3 changes the method of the paper rather than its corpus: every published panel is now re-cut from one committed producer run over the raw captures, so a reader can re-run the number instead of trusting it. That re-cut confirms the descriptive figures on 30 nights and refutes the mechanistic one (§3.2). The direction of travel across three drafts is the same each time: the more of the corpus is reported and the more of the analysis is executable, the weaker the claim that survives.

2. Methods

2.1 Devices and the raw-signal principle

One subject wore a Polar H10 chest strap (single-lead ECG, measured 129.94–130.03 Hz) and a Polar Verity Sense optical armband (measured 176.35–176.42 Hz) simultaneously, captured with Polar Sensor Logger. The re-cut reads the corpus rather than a remembered wear count: 1,980 files walked, 54 Verity _PPG.txt captures offered and 50 paired H10 _ECG.txt, merging by date into 30 scored nights over 2026-06-10 → 2026-07-13. The Verity's three PPG channels are three diametrically-opposed green LED pairs time-multiplexed onto one shared photodiode, plus a fourth LEDs-off ambient slot — not three wavelengths and not three photodiodes (FCC INW4J internal photographs; four Polar staff statements in the vendor SDK issue tracker). Streaming raw PPG forces SDK mode, which disables the device's own HR and PPI outputs, so both vendor files are empty by design and every optical beat here is derived from the raw waveform.

ECG R-peaks come from a Pan–Tompkins implementation with sub-sample parabolic refinement, validated against template cross-correlation at 0.244 ms residual jitter (this figure and the 0.47 ms below are carried from Draft v2; neither is re-cut by this draft’s producer) (median night) versus the 2.213 ms the bare 7.69 ms sample grid would impose, and cross-checked against the H10's own RR file at 0.47 ms median interval agreement. Optical beats come from the production PPGDSP path: per-channel band-pass, TERMA detection with a cadence-adaptive refractory, three-LED consensus (a beat is kept only where ≥2 of 3 channels agree within ±50 ms), and intersecting-tangent foot timing on the reference channel.

2.2 Alignment — why a single global time map is not enough

This is the methodological trap in the study, and it is worth stating plainly because the naive analysis produces confident nonsense. The two devices are independent BLE peripherals with independent crystals, logged by one phone; the phone timestamp is derived from each device's own sensor clock, so same-phone logging does not synchronise them. Fitting one affine map tppg = a·tecg + b over a whole night — offset plus a constant drift rate — yielded a median pulse-transit time of −1 ms (physiologically impossible; the pulse cannot precede the R-wave) and an apparent beat F1 of 0.26, while two nights of the same corpus scored 0.99 under the identical procedure. The failure is not in the signal: restricted to a 20-minute window the same data matches at 1,244 of 1,245 beats with a residual standard deviation of 8 ms.

The cause is that neither the relative clock rate nor the pulse-transit time is constant across seven hours. Alignment is therefore estimated per five-minute epoch: a coarse lag from cross-correlating the two instantaneous-heart-rate series (a slowly-varying signal with no periodicity at the beat interval, so it cannot alias by a whole beat), then a local refinement, then one-to-one beat matching within ±75 ms. Epochs that will not align are excluded by named reason rather than by one summary rule, and every count is in §5: of 2,551 candidate epochs the producer drops 542 for too few paired beats, 0 for no envelope lag and 9 at the jitter floor, leaving 2,000 scored. Because each epoch's lag absorbs the local transit time, the reported pulse-interval bias is near zero by construction and is not evidence about transit time.

2.3 The jitter model

If each optical fiducial carries timing noise of standard deviation σ, that noise propagates into successive-interval differences and inflates rMSSD in quadrature. Writing the measured optical rMSSD as a function of the ECG truth and the jitter gives rMSSD²ppg = rMSSD²ecg + k·σ², with k set by how much fiducial noise the successive-difference operator sees. Fully independent per-beat noise gives k = 6; noise shared between adjacent intervals pulls it toward 2, which is what a foot-to-foot construction predicts since each foot enters two intervals. k is fitted per night by least squares on the epoch series and reported with its distribution, never assumed.

2.4 Statistical treatment

Epochs within a night are not independent. Every inferential statement below is therefore computed per night and summarised across the 30 nights; pooled epoch-level figures appear only as descriptive and are labelled as such. This distinction is not cosmetic — a companion paper (Pooling epochs inverts gate rankings) shows that on this same corpus the pooled and night-clustered analyses rank signal-quality gates in exactly opposite order.

3. Results

3.1 Beat detection is near-perfect; timing is not

Across 30 nights the optical detector finds essentially every ECG beat and invents essentially none: median sensitivity 100.00 % (IQR 99.58–100.00) and median PPV 100.00 % (IQR 100.00–100.00). Pulse-interval bias is +0.01 ms. What the optical arm loses is not beats but timing precision: median jitter 6.34 ms (IQR 4.61–8.83, range 3.74–34.23 across nights).

3.2 The jitter model does not reproduce — Draft v2’s central result, retracted

Draft v2 fitted rMSSD²ppg = rMSSD²ecg + k·σ² per night and reported a median R² of 0.964 (IQR 0.937–0.989) with k = 2.39, concluding that rMSSD error is fiducial jitter and essentially nothing else. Re-cut from the producer over 30 nights and 1,961 epochs, the same fit reads k = 1.21 (IQR 0.44–2.53) and R² = 0.088 (IQR −0.046–0.343, worst −0.651). A negative R² means the fitted line predicts that night’s increment worse than its own mean does. The fitted k now sits below the theoretical band [2,6] rather than inside it, so the one coincidence that made v2’s k persuasive — a free parameter landing in a band predicted from the interval construction — is gone too.

A published R² is ambiguous, so all five candidates were measured rather than assumed. R² for a fit through the origin depends on the total it is compared against, and this model admits three; the equation itself admits a second estimator; and a reader may simply be looking at the devices’ agreement. None of the five reaches 0.964:

The freed fit is the informative one. Its a reads 1.06 (IQR 0.99–1.17), so v2’s a ≡ 1 constraint costs almost nothing — the model is not failing because it was written too rigidly — and even with both coefficients free the fit explains two thirds of rMSSD²ppg, which is roughly what rMSSD²ecg explains on its own. The σ² term is not carrying the paper’s claim. Pooled across all 1,961 epochs the same model gives k = 2.94 with R² = 0.194 (descriptive only, and the pooled k is the one figure that lands inside [2,6]).

Part of the failure is structural, and that part is not a verdict on the physics. σ² is the fit’s only predictor, so a night can only produce a high R² if its jitter actually varies between epochs. Within a night the jitter IQR is a median of 4.82 ms (range 0.55–29.72), against a between-night range of 3.74–34.23 ms: most nights are nearly constant-jitter, which bounds the achievable per-night R² whether the model is right or wrong. A design that wanted to test this model properly would need jitter deliberately varied within a recording — which is also why the pooled fit, spanning the between-night range, behaves better than the per-night ones. What the corpus can support is the association in §3.3, not a coefficient.

Because the model does not hold, the closed-form budget below is no longer a design target. It is retained, re-cut, and labelled, for one reason: it is the inversion Draft v2 published, and a reader comparing drafts needs to see what the same algebra yields once k is measured rather than assumed. 3.3 Catastrophic epochs are high-jitter epochs, not a separate failure mode

283 of 1,961 epochs (14.4%) carry |ΔrMSSD| > 25%. They are not a distinct pathology: their median jitter is 19.2 ms against 5.6 ms elsewhere, and 67% of them exceed 15 ms jitter against 8% of the remainder — a majority, not the clean separation the previous draft reported, so a 15 ms jitter gate would leave a third of them in place. Draft v2 read this as the same model acting at the extreme; with the model refuted (§3.2) the honest statement is weaker and still actionable: jitter and gross rMSSD error are strongly associated in the tail, and the association is what a gate can act on even though the coefficient is not identified. Practically this means the two outlier nights in §3.1 need no separate explanation, and that any intervention which reduces jitter — or any gate that excludes high-jitter epochs — addresses the whole error distribution at once.

3.4 What this does to Draft v1's confidence-gate claim

Draft v1 reported that the pipeline's reference-free gate was “a near-perfect self-classifier,” having labelled two of four nights trusted and two flagged in exact correspondence with the ECG-measured error. That observation stands and reproduces here — but it is a claim about night-level triage resting on a single binary split of four nights, which is consistent with chance. It is not evidence about epoch-level gating, and the two questions have different answers. At epoch level, evaluated within nights, the shipped per-beat SQI discriminates catastrophic epochs with a median AUC of 0.763 (carried from Draft v2 — this draft’s producer does not re-cut the SQI arm) (IQR 0.645–0.791; above chance on 9 of 10 evaluable nights, Wilcoxon p = 0.014) — good, but not “near-perfect,” and materially short of what the v1 phrasing implies. The full gate comparison, including a methodological hazard that inverts the ranking entirely, is the subject of the companion paper.

4. Discussion

Three conclusions follow, and the second is the opposite of Draft v2’s. (1) The optical arm’s weakness on real overnight data is not beat detection — sensitivity and PPV are both at ceiling across 30 nights — but fiducial timing precision. Effort spent finding more beats is misdirected; effort spent locating them more precisely is not. That conclusion needs no model: it rests on the measured 6.34 ms jitter beside a 100% match rate. (2) The accuracy question does not collapse to a single scalar. Draft v2 claimed it did, on a per-night R² of 0.964, and that fit does not reproduce: 0.088 on the same equation, and no more than 0.672 under any of the four alternative estimators in Table 1a. Jitter remains the best single predictor the corpus offers, and the tail association in §3.3 is strong, but a designer cannot be told what a given jitter reduction buys in rMSSD accuracy — and this corpus structurally cannot tell them, because within a night jitter barely varies (IQR 4.82 ms). Establishing or refuting the model needs a protocol that varies jitter deliberately within a recording, which is a different experiment, not more nights of this one. (3) The corpus-honest agreement figure (+4.20% per-epoch median, mean +12.56% with limits of agreement −45% to +71%) is both far worse than the selected-best-nights figure Draft v1 reported (−0.6%) and far better than its own tail suggests. Reporting the median without the tail overstates what a wearable delivers; reporting the tail without the median understates it. Both belong in any accuracy statement, and this revision exists partly to correct the first draft’s omission of the second and the second draft’s over-reading of the mechanism.

A negative result worth recording (both figures carried from Draft v2, not re-cut by this draft’s producer): the two obvious routes to lower jitter were tested on this hardware and both failed. Combining the three LED channels into one waveform — by simple averaging, by the first principal component, or by a maximum-SNR generalised-eigenvalue beamformer — loses to selecting the best single channel, out of sample, on both a spectral criterion and on foot-to-foot interval dispersion; the channels correlate at ρ ≈ 0.99, so there is little independent noise to average away. And substituting the middle-amplitude fiducial that the tilt-table literature ranks above the intersecting-tangent point makes timing worse here (jitter 5.02 ms vs 4.44 ms on a matched segment), consistent with both source papers' own caution that the ranking is morphology- and site-dependent. The jitter floor on this device is therefore not trivially removable, which is precisely why quantifying it is useful.

Limitations. One subject, one device pair. Nothing here supports a between-subject or population claim. The jitter model was fitted across 1,961 epochs as a within-signal physical relationship rather than a between-subject effect, which is why it was reported at all at n=1 — and it failed to reproduce here (§3.2), with part of the failure attributable to the corpus having almost no within-night variation in σ to fit against. So this corpus neither supports the model nor cleanly refutes the physics behind it; it refutes the published claim that the model is established. Every night is quiet sleep, so the motion-regime axis is untested; the two outlier nights show a failure mode but not a graded motion sweep. The ECG reference is itself imperfect (0.244 ms fiducial jitter; 0.47 ms median interval agreement against the device's own RR stream) and contributes a small, un-deconvolved share of the measured σ. Per-epoch alignment absorbs pulse-transit time by construction, so no transit-time result is claimed. Epochs within a night are correlated and all inference is night-clustered; at 30 nights the night-level intervals remain wide, and two nights dominate the spread. Draft v2 reported a Wilcoxon signed-rank p for the night-level bias; this draft reports the sign count (28 of 30 positive) instead, because the producer does not compute a signed-rank test and a p-value without a producer is exactly what this re-cut exists to remove. Finally, the model was fitted and evaluated on the same corpus — a held-out subject or device remains the obvious next test, now alongside a protocol that varies jitter within a recording.

5. Reproducibility

  • Data: simultaneous overnight recordings as Polar_Sense_*_PPG.txt (raw 3-channel + ambient, ~300 MB/night) and Polar_H10_*_ECG.txt, 2026-06-10 → 2026-07-13. The producer walked 1,980 files, was offered 54 Verity captures and 50 paired H10 captures, and merged them by date into 30 scored nights; the wear count is read from the corpus rather than remembered, which is why it replaces Draft v2's stated 26. Raw archives are gitignored for size, and the per-epoch table is not committed — its rows are per-night capture records, which stay off the public repository. The committed artifacts are the producer below and the pooled record it writes.
  • Producer: tools/ppi-jitter-vs-ecg.mjs, run over the capture corpus docs/CORPUS-LOCATIONS.md names. It co-loads the real node realms, re-estimates the per-epoch alignment, matches beats one-to-one, and prints every figure in §3.1 to §3.3 — the per-epoch rMSSD and SDNN bias, the per-night k-fit, the catastrophic split and Table 2's budget. Pooled results, populations and limits are recorded in audits/PPG-ECG-HRV-PAPER-EPOCH-BIAS-2026-10-28.json.
  • Detectors: real ecgdex-dsp.js / ppgdex-dsp.js, run unmodified in a co-loaded realm in PpgDex.src.html script order, so the optical arm is byte-identical to the shipped app (~28 s and ~1.1 GB peak per night).
  • ECG reference: Pan–Tompkins with four documented guards added after observed all-night failures (SPKI latch; search-back feedback runaway), sub-sample parabolic refinement validated at 0.244 ms against template cross-correlation, and an insertion/deletion-tolerant cross-check against the H10 *_RR.txt at 0.47 ms median.
  • Alignment: per-epoch; coarse lag by instantaneous-heart-rate cross-correlation then local refinement; ±75 ms one-to-one matching. Every exclusion is counted by reason rather than summarised: of 2,551 candidate epochs, 542 carried too few paired beats, 0 produced no envelope lag and 9 were refused by the jitter floor, leaving 2,000 scored; of those, 37 crossed a session boundary and 2 were refused by the ECG node, leaving 1,961 with a per-epoch HRV pair.
  • Statistics: all inference night-clustered — a per-night statistic, then an across-night summary. The producer reports medians, IQRs, ranges and counts (how many of the 30 nights fall each side of a bar); it computes no significance test, so this draft quotes none of its own. The one p-value left in the paper (§3.4) is carried from Draft v2 and flagged there; Draft v2’s night-level signed-rank test is replaced by a sign count. Pooled epoch figures are labelled descriptive throughout.

6. Sample size and where returns diminish

Draft v1 projected that within-subject limits of agreement would stabilise at roughly 25–30 trusted paired nights and that the budget should then shift from depth to breadth. At 30 nights this draft sits inside that window and the projection broadly holds for the DESCRIPTIVE half: the night-level bias interval is ±10.6 percentage points at 95% (from the printed mean +12.56% and sd 29.57 over 30 nights) while the limits of agreement remain wide because two outlier nights dominate the spread. Draft v2 added the observation that the jitter model reached its asymptote far earlier than the agreement interval did, on a per-night R² of 0.964 with a tight IQR. That inverts under the re-cut: the model fit is the weakest panel in the paper (R² = 0.088, IQR −0.046–0.343), and more nights will not fix it, because the limiting quantity is within-night σ variance (IQR 4.82 ms) rather than night count. Additional single-subject nights will narrow the agreement interval slowly and will do nothing for the model. The remaining value is therefore in breadth: more subjects, and above all more motion regimes, since every night here is quiet sleep and the jitter distribution under motion is exactly what a wearable-grade claim would need.
